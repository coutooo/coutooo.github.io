How to stop doomscrolling: 9 habits that actually work

You open your phone to check one thing and look up forty minutes later, feeling worse. That's doomscrolling. Willpower alone rarely fixes it, but a few small changes to how your phone works usually do.

By Manuel Couto Updated 15 Nov 2026 7 min read
🌀 📱 🌙

What doomscrolling is (and why it's so sticky)

Doomscrolling is the habit of scrolling through feeds, often bad news or endless short videos, long after it stops being useful or enjoyable. Feeds are built with no natural stopping point: there's always one more post, and the next one might be the interesting one.

The hard part is that it rarely feels like a decision. You don't choose to scroll for 40 minutes. You choose to open the app for "just a second", and the feed does the rest. That's why the most effective fixes work before you open the app, not after.

9 habits that break the loop

  1. Add friction to the worst apps. Move them off your Home Screen, into a folder on the last page. Every extra swipe gives you a moment to notice what you're doing.
  2. Block them at the times you're weakest. For most people that's in bed, first thing in the morning and during work. Schedule blocks for exactly those windows.
  3. Pause before you open. A five-second pause with one honest question, "do I actually want this right now?", is often enough to put the phone back down.
  4. Charge your phone outside the bedroom. Use a cheap alarm clock instead. This single change removes the late-night scroll and the morning one.
  5. Turn off non-essential notifications. Every badge is an invitation back into the feed. Keep calls, messages from people and calendar alerts; turn off the rest.
  6. Replace, don't just remove. Decide what you'll do instead: a book on the nightstand, a podcast for the commute, a walk after lunch.
  7. Use grayscale in the evening. Colour makes feeds more rewarding. Settings → Accessibility → Display & Text Size → Color Filters → Grayscale.
  8. Work in focus blocks. 25 to 50 minutes with distracting apps blocked, then a real break. You'll check your phone less when you know a break is coming.
  9. Make progress visible. Seeing that you got back 1h 20m today is motivating in a way that "use your phone less" never is.

Why "just use it less" doesn't work

Screen time reports tell you what already happened. By the time you see "6 hours a day", the time is gone, and feeling guilty about a number rarely changes behaviour. What works is changing the environment: making the bad option slower and the good option more rewarding.

💡

The trick: you don't need to block everything forever. Blocking your two or three worst apps during your two or three weakest times of day covers most of the damage.

Frequently asked questions

What does doomscrolling mean?

Doomscrolling means scrolling through feeds, often negative news or short videos, for much longer than you intended, usually without enjoying it.

How do I stop doomscrolling at night?

Charge your phone outside the bedroom, schedule a block on your feed apps from an hour before bed, and keep a book or audio on your nightstand to replace the habit.

Do app blockers really help?

Yes, especially when they add a pause or block apps at the times you're most likely to scroll. They work best for your two or three most distracting apps rather than everything.
